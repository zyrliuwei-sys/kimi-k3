## Start with one visual decision

The fastest way to a useful AI image is not a long prompt. It is a clear first decision: what should the image help you communicate? A product launch, a social post, a mood board, and an editorial portrait each need a different subject, frame, and amount of empty space.

Open the [Kimi K3 Image Generator](/image-generator), name the main subject, then add the visual direction around it. A useful prompt normally covers four things:

1. **Subject** — what is present in the frame.
2. **Medium and style** — editorial photography, ink drawing, 3D render, collage, and so on.
3. **Light and composition** — overhead, close-up, soft morning light, or room for a headline.
4. **Constraints** — for example, no text, no logos, or a clean background.

“A ceramic coffee cup” is a subject. “Editorial studio photograph of a ceramic coffee cup on a pale stone plinth, soft side light, 4:5, no text” gives the model a usable direction.

## Choose the frame before you generate

The aspect ratio should match the destination rather than the subject alone. Square images are an efficient default for feeds and product explorations. Vertical frames work for stories, posters, and portraits. Wide frames give a landscape, presentation slide, or web header room to breathe.

Use one output for the first attempt. Once the direction is right, increase the image count to explore variations. This keeps the first pass focused and makes it easier to see which part of a prompt is improving the result.

## Use references for the decisions words cannot carry

Upload a reference when the exact silhouette, material palette, composition, or mood matters. Describe its role in the prompt: “use the uploaded image for the brushed aluminium finish” is more helpful than attaching it without context. References should guide a new image, not replace the need to describe what must change.

## Refine one variable at a time

When an image is close, preserve the successful parts of the prompt and change one or two variables. Try “warmer late-afternoon light,” “closer crop,” or “leave space on the left for copy.” Rewriting every sentence after each attempt makes it hard to learn why an image improved or regressed.

Keep the final checks human: confirm that text is accurate, people and products are represented appropriately, and every input image is one you are entitled to use. Download the result only after it meets the requirements of the place where it will appear.
